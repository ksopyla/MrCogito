E33t · the catalogue read · 30M and 100M models · as of 12 Nov 2026, 08:00 UTC

At 30M our catalogue read matches LSA and leads past 128k; at 100M it trails the LSA port slightly

We changed how the model looks things up in its notebook. The notes no longer get read; they only point to the exact words worth rereading, like cards in a library catalogue. At 30M parameters it now recovers 91 % of what rereading every page gives at 16k tokens (our previous design, E33p notes-open: 39 %), is level with the LSA port (the published LSA read rebuilt on our backbone) at 64k, and after a short extra stage on 64k-token documents predicts 256k-token documents better than any other model we trained. At 100M parameters, trained without that 64k stage, it is 0.02 nats behind the LSA port up to 64k and 0.065 behind at 128k, with general knowledge tied.

The models, their size and their training

All eleven are small language models on one backbone: width 576; two layers, then the reading layer, then five more; the reading layer and one local layer run four times in a loop; the GPT-2 vocabulary of 50,257 tokens. They differ only in how the reading layer reaches text older than the last 512 tokens. Each name starts with its experiment (E33p: our previous notebook and the controls; E33t: the catalogue read), then the run name used in our result files and on W&B.

Training data: the same for every model, in the same order

  1. 1.5B tokens of FineWeb-Edu (educational web pages) in rows of 4,096 tokens; learning rate 1e-3, constant after a 1 % warm-up.
  2. 0.2B tokens of LongDocs-lite in rows of 16,384 tokens: documents of at least 16k tokens from Project Gutenberg books, arXiv papers, DOAB open-access books, US government publications and UK Hansard. The learning rate decays to a tenth, with a fresh optimizer. 1 % of each source's documents are held out for the tests.
  3. Stage C only: 0.1B more tokens of the same long documents in rows of 65,536 tokens (381 steps), learning rate 1e-4 falling to 1e-5. Given to the window control, the LSA port and n4.

Each optimizer step sees 64 rows of 4k tokens (0.26M tokens). 1.7B tokens is about 23 per parameter counting the word tables (56 without), close to the 20 per parameter usually taken as compute-optimal. The 100M run on AWS (stage D) gets 3.4B tokens (3.0B + 0.4B) for 166.4M parameters, 100.6M without the word tables.

How much of the full-reread benefit each model recovers

Think of a student reading a long book. The window control only sees the last few pages (0 %). The dense model may reread any page at any time (100 %). Each model's score is the share of that gap it closes on words more than 2k tokens into a document. Past 16k the dense model is reading longer documents than it was trained on and gets worse, so scores above 100 % are possible there.

What we learned

Stage A

Letting each pass revisit notes closed most of the gap

E33t catalogue made each of its four reading passes look somewhere new: 71 % at 16k. Dropping that rule in training, nothing else changed (catalogue_reuse), lifted it to 91 %. On a planted password the later passes now return to it 95–97 % of the time at 4k, as LSA's do.

Stage A

Two-level search holds at 16k, slips at 64k

Searching pages first and then the notes inside them keeps the search cost growing with pages, not words. It is 3 points behind the flat search at 16k but 16 behind at 64k, well outside the 5 points we allowed. At 256k, after the 64k stage, it keeps 0.11 of the flat search's 0.18 nats.

Stage B

32 or 64 notes per page is a near-tie; 16 is too few

n4 (64 notes) won by the rule written before the runs (88 vs 86 % on the same machine), but the margin is a third of the hardware gap. 16 notes lost 18 points. 512-token pages sat 2–4 points behind, with the best needle-puzzle score.

Stage B

Home and AWS runs differ by about 5 points

The same model, seed and recipe scored 91 % on AWS and 86 % on Odra, and the control models shift too. Comparisons are only fair between models trained on the same machines.

Stages A–C

No harm to general knowledge

Seven short benchmarks average 40.1–41.0 % for every AWS-trained model, the window control 40.8 %. Home-trained models score 38.9–39.9 %, which is the hardware gap again, not the notebook. The 64k stage moved none of them by more than 0.1 point.

Stage C

A short 64k stage helps most past 128k

0.1B tokens of 64k-token documents left the gains at 4k–32k unchanged and lifted them at 128k: from 0.13 to 0.16 nats for n4 (64 notes), from 0.11 to 0.17 for the LSA port. At 256k n4 gains 0.18, LSA 0.16.

Stage C

The gain is the notebook's; passwords stay rare

With its notebook removed, n4 falls just below the window control at every length from 4k to 256k. Passwords: at most 2 of 20 at 128k for any model; at 1M n4 finds none and the LSA port's 1 of 20 is the only full answer that far.

Stage D

At 100M the catalogue trails the LSA port, and its read fades at 128k

Trained the standard way (no 64k stage), n4 is 0.02 nats behind the LSA port up to 64k, about its gap on nearby text, and 0.065 behind at 128k. Its notebook carries 0.15–0.21 nats up to 64k but only 0.065 at 128k. Whether the 64k stage closes this at 100M, as it did at 30M, is untested.

How each test is scored

Line chart · scorecard

Share of the full-reread benefit

We measure the next-word loss: how surprised the model is by each true next token of a held-out long document, in nats (lower is better). Only positions more than 2,048 tokens into a document count, weighted by tokens over the five sources, 8 documents per source at each length. The share is (window loss − model loss) ÷ (window loss − dense loss) × 100: 0 % is no better than seeing the last 512 tokens, 100 % is as good as rereading everything.

Long-document chart

Gain over the window control, 64k to 1M

The same loss as a difference: the window control's loss minus the model's, in nats (higher is better), against a window control trained the same way. At 64k it averages 37 documents, at 128k 21, at 256k four (1.8M scored tokens), and at 1M one document, which makes that column an illustration only.

Scorecard · needle puzzles

RULER: 13 tasks at 4,096 tokens

The RULER benchmark through lm-evaluation-harness, 100 prompts per task, each 4,096 tokens long: find one or several planted key–value pairs in filler text (8 tasks), follow chains of variable assignments (1), name the most common or most frequent words (2), and answer questions about passages (SQuAD, HotpotQA). The model writes its answer greedily, up to 32 tokens; a prompt scores the share of the expected answers that appear in the reply. The column is the mean of the 13 tasks × 100. At 30M parameters most tasks stay near zero (dense 16.0); the easiest, one planted pair, reaches 80–94 % for the best reading models.

Scorecard · general knowledge

Seven short benchmarks, no examples given

lm-evaluation-harness, full test sets, zero-shot: HellaSwag (finish a sentence), ARC-Easy and ARC-Challenge (school science), PIQA (physical common sense), WinoGrande (whom a pronoun means), SciQ (science questions) and LAMBADA (the last word of a passage). In multiple choice the answer is the option the model finds most likely; LAMBADA counts only when the model's top guess is the exact last word. The column is the mean accuracy in %. Guessing gives about 29 % and every model scores 39–41 %, so this checks that the notebook does no harm; it does not test reading.

Long documents · passwords

A pass key hidden in a long real document

A sentence giving a random five-digit pass key is planted at one of 20 depths, from 2.5 % to 97.5 % of a held-out document, and the document ends by asking for the key. We read the model's top guess at each answer position, given the correct digits before it, and count the first digit and all five. At 128k the 20 cases use 17 different documents; at 1M only one document is long enough.

Every number

How much a number can move by chance

One seed per model. The same controls trained twice, with seed and machine both changed, moved by up to 7.6 points of the gap at 16k; the same model, seed and recipe scored 5.2 points lower on Odra than on AWS. Compare models trained on the same machines, and treat gaps under about 5 points as ties.
