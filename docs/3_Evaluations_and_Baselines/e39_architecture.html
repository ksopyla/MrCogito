Living concepts: check the design before it is trained

The bet: the reading layer should group its memory the way text is written. First into spans: runs of neighbouring tokens, cut where the meaning shifts. Then into living concepts: groups of spans that last for the whole document. Today's concept memory uses equal-size groups of scattered tokens inside fixed blocks of 512 tokens.

  • What it should fix. On questions that need several far items at once, concept memory answers 25 in 100 and the topic index 54 in 100 (measured on the 150M models; mean of four cells of RULER, a public set of long-context retrieval tests, at 8k and 16k tokens; higher is better). On single lookups the two tie at 100 in 100, and on plain long text they tie as well.
  • Why we think so is a hypothesis, not a measurement. Equal groups can scatter the tokens of one fact over several groups, and a one-off fact shares its group with about 7 unrelated tokens. A check on the released model tests this before any money is spent.
  • What changes. Only how one layer groups and searches its memory. Same body, same 141,506,208 weights, same training recipe. Nothing is built yet, so nothing on this page about the new design is a result.

What just happened

How to read the outcome. The toy's small budget is the same for all three variants. With its real budget of 512 tokens (about 64 groups), concept memory does open single facts whole: it answers 98 to 100 in 100 single lookups (measured). What the toy shows is the cost: a fact that is split over groups takes several of the reader's picks, and an answer made of several facts takes several times more. Whether that is what holds concept memory back on multi-item questions is the hypothesis the pre-flight check tests. The toy can also fall the other way: on the two-step question, one of concept memory's groups happens to hold "key", "box" and "attic" together, and it answers in a single pass. In pass 2 of the two-step question, the moved question is set by hand; in the model, the local layer inside the loop has to learn it.

4 · The budget: what one reader holds

All three variants give a reader the same exact reads: its raw window and up to 512 opened tokens. They differ in how many summary entries share the reader's attention, and in how many entries it must score to make its choice. The first two quantities do not grow with the document. The last two do, and for living concepts nobody knows yet how fast.

0.50 = grows with the square root of the length · 1.00 = grows as fast as the document

What one reader holds, for the last reader of a document of each length. The raw window and the opened tokens are read exactly. Fewer summary entries leave more of the reader's attention for those exact reads, and fewer entries to score make the choice cheaper.

  • Concept memory: 64 groups per closed block of 512 tokens, so the count follows from the rule at any length. 1,920 and 8,064 are the spec's figures.
  • Spans only: about one span per 8 tokens while the controller holds that average in training (the spec's figure for a whole 16k row is about 2,048 spans). At test time the count follows the text; the hard bounds allow 32 to 128 spans per 512 tokens.
  • Living concepts: about 64 tokens per concept at the training lengths. About 256 at 16k is the spec's figure; about 1,024 at 64k follows from the same ratio. Past 64k the count is unknown. The thresholds are frozen and the count follows the text. The band runs from square-root growth to linear growth. The planned readout measures it: concepts per document at 128k against 64k, on 15 held-out documents.
  • Entries scored, living concepts: every visible concept, plus the spans of the 32 kept ones, assuming kept concepts are of average size. Worked out on this page from the spec's averages; not measured.

What a multi-item answer needs

each about 8 tokens long

The concept-memory row is the diagnosis behind this design, and it is a hypothesis: the pre-flight check counts, on the released model, in how many groups the tokens of each expected answer really sit and how many of them the reader opens. A second possible cause is listed in the spec as a risk: the exact reads share one softmax with the summaries (1,920 of them at 16k in concept memory, about 256 with living concepts), and the topic index, which has no summaries, may win for that reason.

5 · The masks: who may read what

The reading layer cuts every row into strides of 512 tokens. The rule for one reader is the same in all three variants; what differs is what its memory is made of. The row below holds two documents: 16 strides, 8,192 tokens.

  • Own stride and the one before: raw, word for word (512 to 1,023 tokens).
  • Two strides back or more: memory only. Up to 512 tokens opened word for word; the rest through summaries, as the memory stood two strides back.
  • The first 4 tokens of the document (the sinks, a resting place for attention): always.
  • Another document's tokens: never. Concepts never mix documents, and a new document starts with an empty memory.
  • Tokens after the reader: never.
