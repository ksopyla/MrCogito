Notebook vs LSA Read

Our notes-as-index model and LSA's read try to do the same thing: let each word look back at a few hundred exact words chosen from a long document. LSA's read, ported into the same small model, is as good as letting the model reread every page. Ours recovers 39 % of that gain at 16k words, and less as documents grow. This page shows where the information is lost, and how to build a simpler design of our own that closes the gap and scales to 10M words.

Results so far

Every model has 30M parameters and the same layers except the one layer that reads the far past. The score is the next-word loss on long documents (arXiv papers, books, parliament transcripts, government reports), on positions 2,000 words or more into a document, where the far past can help. We express it as a share of the gap between two controls: a model that sees only the last 512 words (0 %) and a model that can reread every earlier word (100 %).

What limits us

Six measured limits, each one a place where information that full attention would use never reaches our reading layer. The numbers come from the training-time instrumentation (averaged over the last steps of the 16k stage) and from a probe on 16 held-out documents.

Analogies: what LSA does, and our counterpart

Think of the document as a library. LSA's librarian sorts every shelf of 512 cards into 64 small boxes of similar cards and writes on each box a label made from its cards; a reader checks the labels and opens boxes until 512 cards are in hand. Our student instead writes 32 summary notes per 256-word page and files each word under one note; the reader checks note headings, opens the words behind the best notes, and also reads every summary. The ideas to borrow are below. The code stays ours.

The new design: notes as a card catalogue

One coherent change to our read, keeping what is ours (a trained writer that reads a whole page and names its contents) and dropping what the measurements show is hurting. The notes stop being reading material and become a catalogue: small, balanced, never empty, organised in two levels, and searched afresh in every pass so that four passes can follow a chain of references. It is simpler than the current read (no summary path, no overlapping pages, no full choice table) and its index cost grows with pages, not with words.

What is new compared with LSA

  • A learned index. LSA groups keys by geometry alone, with no parameters. Our writer sees the whole page in both directions and can group words by meaning (a name and its later pronoun, a defined symbol and its uses). The balanced filing keeps that learned grouping and fixes its holes.
  • Two levels from the start. Pages and notes already form a catalogue with drawers and cards. LSA needs a separate coarse mode to go past a single level.
  • Passes as lookups. Each pass searches only where earlier passes did not look. That is a small, testable form of reasoning over memory: read A, find it points to B, read B.
  • Optional, later: the index learns from the reads. A small extra loss that asks each note's score to predict how much attention its words actually received once opened, so the writer learns what future queries will need.

Page and note sizes

How big should a page be, and how many notes should it hold? Small notes make a sharper index but more entries to score; big pages make fewer entries but blurrier ones. Move the sliders to see the trade-off for one query, from today's 16k documents to 10M words. The defaults are today's settings; LSA's are block 512, 64 boxes of 8 keys, budget 512.

Scores per query count dot products of 64-number keys in the reading layer (9 heads). The reading layer keeps one key and one value of 64 numbers per word (256 bytes in bf16), so its memory grows with document length but stays on one GPU up to about 10M words; the index search is what must not grow with length.

Plan

Each 30M model costs about 5 hours on one 4-card L40S spot machine (about $30): training on all four cards, the three tests side by side. Stage 1's second seed is still running there.
