The sliding-window notebook (E30): what works, what's broken, and where to go next

A review of the design, the code, the recent runs, how we measure learning, related work, and whether this path leads to a small reasoning model with a 1M–10M-token context.

1 · The short answer

Is E30 better than averaging?
Yes
Better on every hard exam: 40 vs 4 bits on the 1024-token chain, 28 vs 9 on the lookalike exam at 128 tokens.
Is the code correct?
Mostly
No bug touched the published numbers. There's 1 leak of future answer tokens (it didn't fire on our rows) and 2 design gaps that likely cap performance.
Is it a new frontier?
No: a strong step
The write is nearly DeepSeek-V4's compressor, reached on our own. What's new is several learned questions per window and the exam harness.
Does it reach 1M–10M?
Not alone
It needs a question-aware fetch and a hierarchy. Every frontier system that works at 1M+ has both.
One sentence: E30 fixed the write (a filter beats an average). The remaining wall is the read. The notebook is written before anyone knows the question, and the reader gets only one glance at it. The next bet should let the question pick pages and then read the original text of those pages. That's "compress to find, fetch to read."

2 · How E30 works, as a picture

Analogy (from the glossary): the text before the question is a book. The reader can't see the book after the QUERY marker. They only see a notebook of notes someone wrote beforehand. E21 wrote one note per 16 tokens by averaging them. E30 lays overlapping pages over the book. Each page has K learned "questions", and each question writes one note by looking up the tokens it cares about.

3 · What the recent runs say

Metric: share of the answer's information recovered. This is "recovered bits ÷ prize bits", so 100% means the model fully knows the answer and 0% is chance. Higher is better. Model size ~31M (width 960, 4 layers). Exam: single lookup (find the value stored under a key in the book).

Book lengths 128 to 4096 tokens, one run each. At 1024 and 2048 the full read itself fails, so those points measure what a model learns within the budget, not what fits in the notebook. The 2048 point is 25 bits (39%) with 128-token pages.

4 · How we test learning, and how much to trust it

What the harness does well

1
Synthetic "DNA" rows with a closed-form floor. You can say exactly how many bits the answer holds (the "prize") and how many a local-only model can get.
2
Recovered bits = (floor − loss) × answer length. This is an honest information measure, and far better than accuracy alone.
3
Channel checks: cut the notebook ("none" → chance) and swap notebooks between rows. Together they prove the notebook carries the answer.
4
Controls: full read (ceiling), local-only (leak check), frozen average (the old write).

Where it's fragile

1
One seed per cell. The "20-bit average" at 16× coverage didn't repeat. Any single-run gap under ~10 bits could be noise.
2
64 eval rows (default --eval_rows 64). That's a few hundred answer tokens, so ±3–5 pp swings are expected.
3
Step-size cliffs decide the outcome (chart below). The same model on the same exam goes from 99% to chance between 1e-4 and 2e-4.
4
The ceiling fails at 1024+. When the full read scores 0, the exam measures trainability, not capacity. The spec's own rule (score only when the full read passes) was waived there.

Step-size cliffs: same architecture, same exam, different step size

With cliffs this sharp, a single step size per cell can rank architectures by which one likes that step size. Fix: 3 seeds × 2 step sizes per cell, report the median, and add "examples to reach 75%" as the main number. You already started logging that metric in the uncommitted change.

A hidden positive: on the 1024 lookup, E30 recovers 26 bits while the uncompressed read gets 0. The notes aren't holding more information than the book does. They're easier to search: 160 notes spread attention less than 1024 tokens do (Hahn 2020, softmax dilution). HiLS (2026) reports the same effect: compressed keys retrieve better because token-level noise averages out. So compression is valuable as an index, even where it fails as a store.

6 · Information flow: the bottleneck isn't where we thought

Capacity isn't the problem

1024-token DNA book: the content is ≈ 2 kbit (4 letters ≈ 2 bits/token). The notebook has 160 notes × (K+V, 64-dim heads, bf16). Even at a pessimistic 1 useful bit per dimension that's ~100× the content. The answer fits many times over, yet we recover ~0.03 kbit. What fails is code design and decodability, not size.

The real dilemma (information bottleneck)

Tishby's rule: keep what predicts the target Y and throw away the rest. But the notebook is written before Y (the question) is known. That leaves two ways out:

A
Universal code: store everything in a form any question can decode. With convex averages and one softmax read, that is hard. Superposed facts interfere. Associative memories (Hopfield / delta rule) are the right tool here.
B
Question-aware read: keep compressed notes only as an index. Once the question is known, fetch the original tokens of the top pages. This is DeepSeek-V4 CSA, NSA, HiLS, MSA, Landmark attention and Quest.

The frontier uses B, plus a heavily compressed "gist" branch for global context (DeepSeek's HCA ≈ our E21 average!).

8 · Honest assessment: frontier or improvement?

What's real and yours

  • A clean result that a learned filter beats an average as the write, where the notebook is the only channel. Most papers never isolate this, because they keep a raw sliding window beside the compressor.
  • The exam harness (recovered bits, channel cuts, the exclusive mask) is more rigorous than most compression papers. It's arguably the most publishable asset.
  • Evidence that compression helps learning to find things (E30 beats the full read at 1024).

What's not new

  • Overlapping windows + softmax pooling + positional bias is DeepSeek-V4's CSA compressor. Several learned queries per window is Q-Former / Perceiver-resampler applied per chunk.
  • Question-blind compression capping retrieval is a known result (ICAE, gist tokens, Fine-KV, DeepSeek-OCR: ~97% at 10×, ~60% at 20×).
  • Our ledger shows the same curve: fine at ≤5×, partial at 6–8×, dead at 11×+ per note.
Verdict: E30 is a big improvement inside our programme (it unblocked the averaging wall), not a frontier architecture. On its own, a question-blind notebook can't reach 1M+ with exact retrieval. That's a structural limit, not a tuning issue. The frontier move is to make the concept layer the index and the controller: notes decide what to read, then the model reasons over what it read, in loops. A small model that learns this end-to-end at 4–32K and extrapolates to 1M+ doesn't exist in the open literature below ~300M parameters. That would be novel.

9 · Can this reach a small 1M–10M-context reasoning model?

Cost per question, per global layer

Log scale. One flat notebook (C = N/8) still grows linearly: 1.25M notes at 10M tokens. A 3-level hierarchy with top-k fetch stays at ~10⁴ operations per question at any length.

What it would take

1
Hierarchy: pages → chapters → volumes (8× each level). The question descends top-k at each level: log-depth, like a B-tree.
2
Train short, run long: train at 4–32K and extrapolate 64–256× (HiLS shows 64× at 345M). Nobody can train a small model at 10M directly.
3
Loops: the answer to a chain needs k hops, and each hop is a new fetch (E19 looped refinement plus TRM-style deep supervision).
4
Scope: a small model reasons over given text. It can't hold world knowledge. That's fine, and it's the RLM / BABILong niche.

Feasibility: 1M is realistic with this design. 10M is realistic for retrieval-style reasoning and speculative for dense global reasoning ("summarise everything"), which has no known efficient solution.

10 · The Jev question: is "output only a decision" the right pivot?

What Jev is

A closed product (Sep 2026): typed answers (choice ≤255 / score / yes-no) with calibrated probabilities, in one parallel pass. No paper or weights. Claims come from the company. An open clone already reads option logits from a 4B LM. The founder says the moat is data, not architecture.

As a pivot
No

It isn't an architectural idea you can research. It's a product interface on top of any encoder. Pivoting would abandon priorities 1–3 (reasoning core, long context, bandwidth).

As something to borrow
Yes

A decision head is the natural first product for a concept encoder. You don't have to generate text, only read a long input and answer with a calibrated choice. That's much easier than fluent generation and still tests the compression.

Concrete use: (1) Add a typed/calibrated readout (choice logits + Brier score / ECE) to the DNA and CogitoProbe exams: cleaner than token CE for multi-token answers. (2) Later milestone: "read 1M tokens, return a calibrated decision" (contract review, log triage, routing). Nobody serves this cheaply, and a small concept model is the right shape for it. VL-JEPA (predict the answer embedding, decode only if asked) is the research-grade version of the same idea and fits the "decoder = crystallisation" vision.
